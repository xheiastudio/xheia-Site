# Proof of Work for a Solo Data Consultant's Website: Should You Build a "Track Record" Page?

## TL;DR
- **Yes to a small "Selected Work" / "Case Studies" section, but no to a "Track Record" page as you're imagining it.** Build a short set of case studies framed around the *client's* problem and measurable outcome — do **not** publish a chronological "engagement at Company X, engagement at Company Y" list. That resume-style list is the most consistently discouraged pattern in solo-consultant website advice, because it makes *you* the subject when the buyer wants to see *their* problem being solved.
- **The four dominant proof-of-work patterns among genuine one-person consultancies are: (1) a handful of narrative problem→approach→result case studies; (2) testimonial-led sections; (3) a "clients include / previously at" logo or credibility strip; and (4) no dedicated proof page at all, replaced by strong positioning plus content (writing, talks, open source).** CV-style project lists are common on *developer/engineer* portfolios but are treated as an anti-pattern for *consultants* selling outcomes.
- **Your specific situation — confidentiality constraints and no testimonials yet — has a well-established solution: the anonymized, outcome-led "mini case study"** ("A UK life-sciences firm cut month-end reporting from 3 days to 2 hours"). You keep the concrete result, drop the client name, and reinforce credibility with visible expertise (an article, a talk, a code sample) and your prior sector/employer track record — then swap in named, testimonial-backed case studies as soon as you're permitted.

## Key Findings

1. **A chronological "Track Record" list reads like a CV — and that's the failure mode to avoid.** The core reframe recommended across consultant-marketing writing is to move from "here's where I've worked" to "here's the problem I solve and the results I produce." The client should be the hero of each story; the consultant is the guide who delivered the transformation.

2. **3–5 curated case studies is the widely cited sweet spot for a solo consultant** — enough to show range and repeatability, few enough to keep quality high and relevant to the specific buyer. The recommended structure for each is Challenge → Approach → Result, with at least one quantified outcome per study.

3. **Testimonials and client logos help, but the outcome is what actually sells.** Logo strips ("trusted by") and short quotes are common and low-effort credibility boosters, but a specific, quantified result inside a short narrative is consistently rated more persuasive than a generic praise quote.

4. **A significant number of respected solo consultants run no dedicated proof page at all**, leading instead with tight positioning plus a steady output of content (newsletter, articles, conference/meetup talks, open-source). This is a legitimate model — especially for referral-driven consultants — but it requires the content engine to exist.

5. **The confidentiality + no-testimonials problem is solvable without inventing anything.** The accepted playbook: anonymize the client but keep the concrete result; borrow credibility from prior employers and sectors; demonstrate competence through public work (a written teardown, a talk, a repo, a sample analysis); publish your engagement method to reduce perceived risk; and actively collect your first one to three testimonials (including from small or pro-bono work) to replace anonymized placeholders over time.

## Details

### Dedicated page vs. folded into About vs. skipped — the verdict
For a solo data consultant actively marketing her site, the right answer is a **dedicated but lightweight "Work" or "Selected Work" area**, not a long "Track Record" and not nothing. A dedicated page signals professionalism and — critically — gives *referrers* something concrete to forward when they recommend you. But it should hold a **small, curated set of outcome-led stories**, not an exhaustive engagement log. Folding one flagship result into the About page is an acceptable minimum viable version if you're time-constrained. Skipping proof entirely only works when positioning and content are doing the credibility work instead.

### Patterns among solo/independent consultants and their rough prevalence
- **Narrative case studies (problem→approach→result):** the recommended gold standard; common among consultants who actively market their sites.
- **Testimonial-led section:** very common, usually as a supplement rather than the whole strategy.
- **Logo / credibility strip ("clients include" / "previously at"):** common and low-effort; works even with anonymized or sector-level descriptors.
- **No dedicated proof page (positioning/content-led):** notably common among senior, referral-driven solo consultants.
- **CV-style chronological project list:** common on *developer/engineer* portfolios; discouraged for *consultants*, where it's treated as an anti-pattern.

These are directional patterns drawn from established consultant-marketing and web-design practice, not from a formal survey (see Caveats).

### Why the CV-style list is specifically wrong for consultants
A resume list answers "who employed you?" — the wrong question for a services buyer, who is asking "can you solve *my* problem, and what will happen if I hire you?" A chronological list also invites unhelpful scrutiny (gaps, short stints, unfamiliar logos) and buries the single element that actually converts: the outcome. The consulting-marketing framing is consistent — the website should dramatize the *client's* before-and-after, not enumerate the consultant's employment history.

### Bridging the credibility gap (confidentiality + no testimonials)
- **Anonymized, quantified mini case studies:** "A FTSE 100 retailer" or "A UK public-sector body" + the measurable result. You keep the proof and lose the name.
- **Results before names:** lead with the metric and the method; the identity is secondary.
- **Borrowed / transferred credibility:** name prior *employers* (where contracts permit) as "previously at," plus relevant certifications and the sectors you've served.
- **Demonstrated expertise as a proxy for social proof:** a technical article, a meetup/conference talk, an open-source repo, or a public sample analysis shows *how you think* — which for early-stage consultants can be more persuasive than a thin testimonial.
- **Process/methodology transparency:** a clearly named engagement process (discovery → build → handover, etc.) reduces the buyer's perceived risk precisely when you lack social proof.
- **First-testimonial tactics:** offer a small paid or pro-bono engagement in exchange for a testimonial; ask for a LinkedIn recommendation; capture the result the moment a project closes, while it's fresh.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Now (early, confidentiality-bound, no testimonials):**
- Create one dedicated page labelled **"Work"** or **"Selected Work"** — avoid the labels "Track Record," "CV," and "Portfolio," which prime the resume reading you're worried about.
- Populate it with **2–4 anonymized, outcome-led mini case studies** spanning your sectors (life sciences, public sector, retail, education, fitness). Use the Challenge → Approach → Result structure and include one quantified result each. Use sector descriptors ("a UK life-sciences company"), not client names.
- Add a **"sectors served / previously worked with"** strip using industry descriptors, or former-employer logos where your contracts allow.
- Link **one piece of public proof** (a short article or a small demo/repo) from the page to substitute for missing testimonials.
- On the About page, restate positioning and surface a single headline result.

**Stage 2 — After 1–3 engagements grant permission:**
- Replace one anonymized study with a **named, testimonial-backed case study** — the strongest single asset you can own.
- Add a lightweight **testimonials** block, seeded with LinkedIn recommendations.

**Stage 3 — Established:**
- Curate down to your **best 3–5 case studies** aligned to the buyer you most want to attract; retire weaker or dated ones.
- If referrals come to dominate your pipeline, you can safely minimize the proof page and reinvest that effort in positioning and content.

**Thresholds that would change this plan:**
- If you obtain permission to name even one marquee client, build that named case study immediately — it outranks everything else.
- If your inbound is essentially 100% referral, downgrade the proof page to a single flagship result and prioritize a content/positioning engine instead.
- If a single sector becomes your clear niche, drop the multi-sector spread and specialize the case studies to that buyer.
